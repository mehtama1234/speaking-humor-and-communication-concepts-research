My name is Yinka Ilory, an artist,
designer, a storyteller, a dreamer,
and a person who loves color.
I believe that my calling and mission
is to bring joy to the world.
We live in a time where
everyone's asking the same question:
"Who am I and what brings me joy?"
Looking back now, I realize this question
has followed me my whole life.
While joy was always present in my life
and always around me,
I just didn't have the language for it.
But what I did have
was a world rich of color, symbols,
dreams and affirmation.
I grew up in London in 27 Ashby House,
on a council estate.
I was raised by Nigerian parents
who moved to London in the 1980s.
My mother from Ondo State,
coined the Sunshine State.
It's known for its majestic
rock landscapes and warm weather.
The people there are warm,
grounded and strong.
My father from Ekiti State
in southwest Nigeria,
a place known for its nature
and famous for its warm and cold springs,
a place called Ikogosi,
and known as the Fountain of Knowledge
because of its reputation
for academic excellence
and intellectual achievement.
Even before I'd been to these places,
I could picture myself growing up there,
being raised there.
But I was born and raised in London.
I felt a strong sense of connection
and a strong sense of belonging
to a place I'd call home.
In the UK, my beloved mother
did many things.
She bought and sold Swiss voile lace.
She catered for weddings and birthdays.
She was an architect of joy,
architect of bringing people
and things together.
In our glass cabinet at home,
she would collect objects of memories
she treasured in her show glass.
My dad worked in retail.
Everyone called him Uncle Paul.
He was everyone's uncle,
dad, brother, mentor and therapist.
He brought so much joy to the workplace.
At one point, my parents also owned
a corner shop in London.
It was insane.
We had all the kids after school,
people in the estate, the neighbors,
passersby would come to the corner shop.
This place became a gathering point
for the whole community.
On Sundays, my mum and dad
would host church services.
They would lead lectures and sermons.
The words of worship and affirmation,
singing and moving
have manifested in my memory.
My parents loved house parties,
live music, plates of jollof rice
weaving in our small council flat.
Different languages. Different journeys.
All in one room.
My parents brought all these rituals
of community and faith
and pleasure with them
from Nigeria to the UK.
As a child, I didn't realize
I was experiencing a daily baptism of joy,
a joy that had been passed on
from generation to generation
without even knowing it.
Every ritual, every gathering,
every exchange
carried a deeply symbolic meaning.
All these were seeds of joy.
How did I harvest joy?
Myself, I developed, like my mother,
the same collective spirit.
She collected objects.
I collected symbols.
A sun, a rock,
hands, a star, a talking drum.
At first I didn't know
why I was drawn to these symbols,
but I realized these symbols
were memories or things I enjoyed
and things that brought me joy as a youth.
The Ondo sunlight,
the rituals, the community.
The gatherings.
Visualizing them helps me harvest joy.
Visual storytelling is how
we unlock stories we already carry.
This is what I do with my work.
I don't invent stories.
I uncover them.
I reimagine them through my lens,
similar to how I reflect
on my personal experiences.
I listened to people's stories
and experiences
and turn them into symbolic symbols.
Because when you visualize something,
when you share it,
you can pass it on to someone else,
another generation.
This is why I think
joy is transgenerational.
We can inherit it from what it was before
or what surrounds us.
This is what I did in a recent experiment.
I asked my Instagram audience,
what does joy look like for you today?
And what are your early memories of joy
looking like to you today?
And how could they be
translated into symbols
that connect with your memories?
Receiving an abundance of responses
felt like I was receiving
another daily baptism of joy,
just like my parents' rituals.
I selected some of the answers
and turned them into symbols.
A mushroom, a cashew tree,
a sports car,
a palm wine cup,
and I paired them up
with symbols from my archive.
Ripples of laughter.
Precious joy.
Connection, sunrise
and community.
This selection was based
on what happened the most.
Around 80 percent of the responses
carried a sense of togetherness
through connection:
shared, inherited, not individual.
Around 65 percent of responses
were connected to nature:
plants, sunlight, open space,
feeling through light, texture and smell.
About half of the responses
spoke about freedom,
movement, exploration, anticipation,
no restrictions.
Some were talking about growth
not always being immediate.
Sometimes it was over time,
sometimes through struggle.
A small number spoke about stillness.
So I took all of these impressions
and turned them into an artwork,
an artwork that celebrates
the power of transgenerational joy.
When it comes together,
this is the magic you can create.
But what stood out to me the most
was that no one spoke
about personal achievement.
It was always about people
and places, and shared experiences.
All of my responses
were very visual already.
It's something we often
recognize in hindsight.
To me, harvesting transgenerational joy
means finding the seeds
that were planted before you,
by your parents, by your loved ones,
by your community,
even by your younger self.
We all carry those seeds.
To be able to see them,
we need to visualize them.
We harvest them by sharing them.
My parents always said to me,
whatever you sow is what you shall reap.
I hope today you will reap joy.
Pass it on from person to person,
from generation to generation.
It was passed on to me
and now I want to pass it on to you.
Thank you.
(Applause)
