On October 4, 1997,
I walked into a room
unlike any room I'd ever entered before.
People were standing up,
sharing their stories.
A wave of relief washed over me.
I got a cup of coffee
and just took everything in.
At that point,
I was at the lowest point in my life.
I was out of ideas,
and I didn't know how I was going
to make it out of the condition I was in.
Before that date,
my life had not been easy.
When I was 25 years old,
I gave birth to a son.
We called him K.K.
The next thing that happened
changed my life forever.
He was struck by a car.
That is the day I lost him.
At only five years old,
he was gone.
His laughter, his love, his energy
were all taken from me.
My life became a downward spiral.
I began to drink.
Drinking led to substance use.
For the substance use, I was imprisoned.
Every time I left prison,
I swore I wouldn't go back.
But I did. Over and over and over again.
I remember one day,
standing in front of a judge
and asking that judge,
"Is there any help for me?"
And the judge sentenced me
to prison one more time.
No one cared.
No one was listening.
But when I walked into that room in 1997,
I was ready to be heard.
In the rooms of Alcoholics Anonymous,
they listened.
I took a deep breath and stood up,
and said, "Look what drugs and alcohol
have done to me."
The immediate compassion,
empathy and love
that rolled off these complete strangers
was enough to put a sizable dent
in my pain, my shame,
my guilt and all that sorrow.
I kept talking,
and they kept listening.
I shared all the thoughts
I had spent decades trying to silence.
As they listened, I began to heal.
Listening saved my life,
and it opened a door.
I got sober in Santa Monica,
an affluent part of Los Angeles.
And I noticed something there.
People in Santa Monica
weren’t going to prison
for substance use,
for alcoholism.
And I thought,
why didn't that judge
give me a paper and say,
"Miss Burton,
go to AA.”
Was I not worth a piece of paper?
Was I not worth
direction toward a solution?
So as I got stronger in my recovery,
I thought about women just like me,
cycling in and out of prison,
looking for safety and not finding it.
So I got a job.
A minimum wage job.
And I saved $13,000.
And I took $3,000 of it
and put down on a house.
And I bought some furniture.
Every morning I’d wake up,
and I'd go down
to the Greyhound bus station
where women leaving prison
were dropped off.
And I knew that station well
because I had been dropped off there
so many times myself.
And as they got off the bus
carrying a trash bag of belongings
and hope in their hearts,
I would walk up to them and I'd say,
"Girl, I have a house.
And if you want to come live there,
it's a safe place to be.
You can come live there."
And before long there were 11 of us
living in that house.
We cooked together.
We went to meetings together.
We shared resources.
We laughed together.
We cried together.
But most of all, we healed together.
Before long, people saw what I was doing,
and they joined me.
And we created a team.
Today, there [are] 12 houses
across Los Angeles.
(Applause)
Thank you.
What we built was a house
of women helping women.
Each house is staffed
to support its residents
who pay rent,
do chores,
buy groceries
and learn to be accountable,
responsible members of the community.
We help them find jobs,
learn financial literacy
and help them with any legal issues.
As a result, thousands of women
have come back to the community
and hundreds of mothers
have been reunited with their children.
In 2017, I published my memoir,
"Becoming Ms. Burton:
From Prison to Recovery
to Leading the Fight
for Incarcerated Women."
In order to tell my story,
I held book talks all over the nation,
behind prison walls.
There I would listen to women.
They were pleading for a place to go
after they were released.
They were sitting with tears
streaming down their faces,
wanting to rebuild their lives,
be reunited with their children,
find a job in their community [and]
break free from
the criminal justice system.
Every year in this country,
around two million women
are released from prisons and jails.
They walk out with
a trash bag of belongings,
hoping someone will listen.
Hoping someone will help.
When there's no help,
when there's no safe place to land,
too many of them will go back.
I knew I could not provide resources
of a new way of life
to all those women.
So I created SAFE,
Sisterhood Alliance
for Freedom and Equality,
to replicate my model.
Our first training was with 13 women.
To date, we've trained over 700.
(Applause)
To listen.
To create support.
To give hope.
When women like me have safe housing,
families reunify,
recidivism drops
and communities stabilize.
I started with one house
of women helping women.
Today, there is an entire network
of women helping women
in 20 US states and also in Uganda,
Kenya and Nigeria.
(Applause)
We have replication down to a science.
The leadership is there,
the demand is there,
and the goal is clear.
More houses in more cities.
More mothers reunited with their children.
More women breaking a cycle
that has lasted generations.
I believe we all share a responsibility
to heal the harms caused
by the criminal justice system.
This is public safety.
This is family preservation.
This is community repair.
When someone listened to me
with compassion rather than judgment,
it changed my life.
My story since that day in 1997,
shows what is possible
when people have support,
hope
and determination.
I cannot bring my son back.
But I know every time a woman
walks through the doors and finds safety,
every time a child
is reunited with his mother,
every time a woman breaks free
from a cycle that once held me,
his legacy goes way beyond his years.
I believe he's smiling down on my efforts,
and I hope he's proud of his mother.
