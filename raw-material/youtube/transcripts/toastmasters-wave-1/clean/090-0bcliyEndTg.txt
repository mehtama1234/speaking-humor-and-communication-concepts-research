One: Know your subject.
Now, if you want to overcome nervousness,&nbsp;&nbsp;
the first guideline is simply to know what you're&nbsp;
talking about. You'll have an easier time giving a&nbsp;&nbsp;
speech on a subject if you know the subject well.
Want to speak about your favorite musician?&nbsp;&nbsp;
Read verified information about them that&nbsp;
you can find online or at your library.&nbsp;&nbsp;
When you become an authority on your topic, the&nbsp;
audience will want to learn what they can from&nbsp;&nbsp;
you. And, you won't have to struggle to keep your&nbsp;
messages straight if you know the message well.&nbsp;
Once you've mastered your topic, you&nbsp;
need to master your presentation.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Make sure you rehearse the entire speech&nbsp;
so you will be comfortable presenting it,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and practice any difficult parts&nbsp;
repeatedly until they flow smoothly.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Whether or not you memorize each word, you should&nbsp;
make sure you know the order of your messages.&nbsp;&nbsp;
That's why new Toastmasters members&nbsp;
begin with the Icebreaker Speech;&nbsp;&nbsp;
it's a chance to talk about the&nbsp;
subject you know best--yourself!&nbsp;
Then, all you have to focus on is&nbsp;
practicing how well you tell your story.
Two: Know your audience and your space
Understand the importance of knowing who&nbsp;&nbsp;
will be listening to you and where you&nbsp;
will be when you give your presentation.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Both the audience and location can create&nbsp;
limitations that are important to understand.&nbsp;
For example, a researcher might speak&nbsp;
about the math behind their work.&nbsp;&nbsp;
If they're addressing a group of college&nbsp;
professors, they could use highly technical&nbsp;&nbsp;
language to discuss a sophisticated project.&nbsp;
But would they want to do that if they were&nbsp;&nbsp;
describing their work to a group of elementary&nbsp;
students? Of course not! For young kids,&nbsp;&nbsp;
it would be better to talk about how&nbsp;
we learn through the research process.
Knowing your audience means understanding and&nbsp;
respecting their particular characteristics.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Before you prepare your speech, their geography,&nbsp;
politics, culture, age, and other traits will&nbsp;&nbsp;
define them as a group and will guide you in&nbsp;
tailoring your presentation to fit their needs.&nbsp;
Preparation reaches beyond&nbsp;
understanding your audience.&nbsp;&nbsp;
You need to also consider how&nbsp;
they will experience your speech.&nbsp;
For example, when you present to an in-person&nbsp;
audience, be sure to check out your location&nbsp;&nbsp;
before the event begins. Will there be enough&nbsp;
space on stage for everything you need?&nbsp;&nbsp;
If you're planning to use any media that requires&nbsp;
a monitor or a screen, is the venue aware?&nbsp;&nbsp;
Do they have everything you require?
If your presentation will be online or hybrid,&nbsp;&nbsp;
have you learned everything you need&nbsp;
to know about the app you will use?&nbsp;&nbsp;
Can you access chat? Will it be turned off, or&nbsp;
will someone else facilitate that feature for you?&nbsp;
All these questions are important to&nbsp;
answer before you start your presentation.&nbsp;
Three: Keep Going
There s no need to apologize&nbsp;&nbsp;
If you make a minor mistake, such as skipping a&nbsp;
portion of your speech or forgetting a few words.&nbsp;&nbsp;
If something like this happens, don't worry. Your&nbsp;
audience most likely doesn't know the difference.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Just move on and you'll find your way back on&nbsp;
track with your next point. If you apologize,&nbsp;&nbsp;
you call attention to the mistake, which takes&nbsp;
away the important point you want to make.
An advanced speaker knows how to guide&nbsp;
the audience's attention toward the&nbsp;&nbsp;
important parts, and away from the small stuff.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Whether you make a mistake or not, just&nbsp;
remember that people want you to succeed,&nbsp;&nbsp;
and because of that, they're rooting for you.&nbsp;
It helps to remember this with the next tip.&nbsp;
Four: Imagine yourself giving a great speech.
A great way to find success is to visualize it.&nbsp;&nbsp;
Give yourself time for this important form of&nbsp;
preparation. See yourself making clear, valid&nbsp;&nbsp;
points with appropriate gestures and body language&nbsp;
that emotionally connects you with your audience.&nbsp;
Once you see yourself using what works,&nbsp;
you'll feel more relaxed and confident.&nbsp;&nbsp;
And don't forget to imagine pausing&nbsp;
while your audience applauds wildly!&nbsp;
Five: Focus on your message, not on yourself&nbsp;
Some beginning speakers let their nervousness&nbsp;
win because they make the mistake of focusing&nbsp;&nbsp;
entirely on themselves. When something goes&nbsp;
wrong, for example, your reaction can cause&nbsp;&nbsp;
your voice to shake. Then, all you hear is a shaky&nbsp;
voice that becomes the focus of your thoughts.&nbsp;
Remember, you're giving a talk for a reason.&nbsp;
Keep that reason in mind and focus on what's&nbsp;&nbsp;
really important your message.
Find the passion to entertain,&nbsp;&nbsp;
educate, inspire, or motivate your audience,&nbsp;
and you'll find a purpose and your voice.
