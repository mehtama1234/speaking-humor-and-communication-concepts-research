# Sources, examples and evidence

This guide is an authored selection from a larger speaking-research collection. It is not a claim that every video in that collection has been watched, transcribed accurately or analyzed end to end. Its evidence rests on the particular local transcript passages identified in each approach chapter.

## Three kinds of material

A source reading explains what a speaker teaches or what appears in a selected example. That may be instruction, an interview, a retrospective account, a staged demonstration or a fictional television scene. Those forms are identified where they affect how the example should be understood.

An original application is written for this guide. Its people, dialogue, operational details and outcomes are invented unless explicitly stated otherwise. It shows a choice in context; it does not document an organization or demonstrate that a technique produced a measured result.

A practice adaptation combines ideas into something a learner can try. The sequence, timings and review questions are editorial designs, not an endorsed or experimentally validated program from the source speakers. A favorable response in one rehearsal is feedback about that attempt, not proof of universal effectiveness.

## What the transcripts can and cannot establish

Text can support close reading of wording, structure and attributed instruction. It cannot by itself establish facial expression, pitch, pause duration or audience reaction. Chapters about delivery therefore ask learners to inspect their own recordings rather than claiming acoustic analysis of the source videos.

Automatic transcripts can contain recognition errors and production annotations. Local line numbers locate passages in those files; they are not video timestamps. Where a chapter inspected only selected passages, its source trail says so. A complete transcript reading is still not a complete audiovisual review or independent verification of every claim made in the source.

Reported business results, personal anecdotes and research findings remain attributed reports unless independent verification is explicitly supplied. Fictional and staged scenes are illustrations, not behavioral evidence. Rewritten dialogue proposed by a commentary channel must not be mistaken for something the featured person actually said.

## Attribution and public sources

The source index below connects each cited transcript to its originating video's title, channel and public address from the local acquisition metadata. Links identify provenance; current availability is not verified by the build. A title may make a stronger promotional promise than this guide adopts.

Follow the chapter links beside each source to find the inspected passage ranges, qualifications and teaching adaptation. The research-workspace edition also links to the local transcript. A portable reader need not include third-party transcripts to retain the source attribution and explanation of inspection scope.

Channel and speaker names identify sources, not sponsorship or endorsement. This guide does not claim ownership of their videos, books, transcripts or trademarks. It contains original instruction and paraphrased commentary; third-party audio, video and imagery are not part of the intended reading package. No sales, hosting or licensing commitments are made on the owner's behalf.

## How to use a disagreement with the source

Some chapters intentionally narrow or revise a source's framing. Examples include preserving legitimate uncertainty when editing softeners, allowing notes during rehearsal, treating respect as a relationship rather than a status performance, and acknowledging that a sensible objection may end a proposal. The chapter explains the distinction rather than silently attributing the revised lesson to the original speaker.

You can follow the same discipline in your practice: borrow a useful move, keep its limits visible and judge the result in the situation where you actually use it.
