# 03 — Give the answer you can stand behind

*Pause, frame, then answer*

The difficult question often arrives with an invisible second question attached. Someone asks, “Can we support twice as many customers?” You hear, “Are you competent enough to be standing here?” Now you are tempted to answer the second question by sounding certain about the first.

That is how a reasonable uncertainty becomes an unreliable promise.

The alternative is to treat the question as a request for usable information, not a referendum on your worth. Pause long enough to identify what is being asked. Then separate the answer you possess from the answer you would have to invent.

## The principle: an answer has a boundary

Knowing something about a subject is not the same as knowing the answer to every question about it. You may know that a test passed without knowing whether the test represents normal use. You may know the direction of a change without knowing its size. You may know who owns an investigation without knowing its result.

A useful response makes those boundaries visible. It tells the listener what they can rely on and what remains unsettled. This does not require a long disclaimer. Often it requires one accurate distinction.

“The test passed” and “We have proved the system can handle the launch” are different claims. If the evidence supports only the first, fluency does not entitle you to say the second.

The pause is a working space in which to notice that difference. It is not a dramatic performance of authority. You do not have to hold the room in silence for a prescribed number of seconds; you need enough space to choose an honest answer.

## The source example: three useful ways not to know

In Alexander Lyon's *How To Say “I don't know” With Credibility!*, the first teaching section offers three responses to missing information: investigate and follow up, refer the question to an appropriate person, or explain the relevant information you do have.

The examples make the third option concrete. Asked for an exact attendance figure, a speaker might not know the number but might know that the venue was full and people were waiting outside. That observation addresses the scale of the event without supplying an invented count. It would not, however, answer a question that genuinely requires a count.

Lyon also qualifies the referral. Choose someone likely to know; do not turn a handoff into blame. If they are present, invite their contribution rather than treating them as a prop in your escape from an uncomfortable question.

Later, he offers versions that omit the explicit words “I don't know.” That can reduce repetition, but it introduces an editorial judgment: would the shorter version conceal an important uncertainty? Our rule is to keep the boundary audible whenever the listener could otherwise mistake related information for a complete answer.

The point is not to become so smooth that nobody notices a gap. It is to make the gap manageable without disguising it.

## A worked application: capacity at launch

*This software-launch exchange is an original teaching example. All project conditions are invented.*

> “Can we support twice as many customers on Monday?”
>
> “Yes, I think we should be fine. The tests looked good.”

This answer takes two uncertain steps without showing either: from unspecified tests to real customer behavior, then from encouraging results to a commitment.

A more useful answer:

> “We passed the test at twice the current request rate. We have not tested the reporting jobs running at the same time. So I can confirm the first result, but not full Monday capacity. I can bring the combined-test result to tomorrow's readiness review.”

The speaker answers the part supported by evidence and identifies the missing condition. The follow-up belongs in a real decision process, not an indefinite “I'll get back to you.” In actual use, promise that result only if the test and timing are feasible. Otherwise commit to an update on the investigation, not a result you may not have.

Suppose the questioner pushes:

> “I need a yes or no. Should we announce it?”

The response now needs a recommendation:

> “Not on the evidence we have today. My recommendation is to wait for the combined test before making that commitment.”

Notice the change. The factual uncertainty remains, but the speaker is not using it to avoid a decision. You can be clear about what to do while remaining honest about what you know.

## Choose the right kind of frame

If the question is ambiguous, resolve the ambiguity: “Do you mean the implementation date or the date customers can start using it?” This is worthwhile when the distinction changes the answer, not as a habitual way to buy time.

If you know only part, identify the part: “I can speak to the support workload; I don't have the infrastructure estimate.” Then give relevant information within that scope.

If you need another person's expertise, make a respectful handoff: “Maya owns the test results. Maya, can you speak to the latest run?” Do not imply that Maya has approved something you have not discussed.

If the information is unavailable, say so. No arrangement of words can turn an unmeasured quantity into a known one. A useful next step may be to explain how it could be measured or why the decision must proceed without it.

## When this goes wrong

Framing becomes evasion when it replaces the question with an easier one. Asked whether the deadline was missed, a speech about the team's dedication is not a partial answer. Answer the factual question first.

Follow-up becomes theater when it lacks ownership. Keep a record of the question, the person responsible and the agreed update point. If you promised to return, return even when the update is that the work remains incomplete.

The pattern also fails when caution expands into a paragraph of qualifications before any useful information appears. Lead with the best-supported answer, then state the limit that matters. The aim is not maximal hedging; it is accurate scope.

## Practice: answer, limit, next step

Choose a real topic you may be questioned about. Write three questions: one you can answer, one you can answer partly and one you cannot answer. For each, note the evidence available before rehearsing any wording.

Answer aloud. For the partial answer, make the distinction between known and unknown understandable in one pass. For the unanswered question, offer a feasible next step or say plainly that you cannot supply one. Do not manufacture a follow-up just to complete the pattern.

Listen back and ask: did I answer the actual question; did I imply more certainty than the evidence supports; and could the listener tell what happens next? If a partner can correctly repeat both your answer and its boundary, you have a useful indication that this attempt was clear.
