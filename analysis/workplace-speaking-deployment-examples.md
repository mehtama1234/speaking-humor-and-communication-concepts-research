# Workplace Speaking Deployment Examples

Concrete workplace examples that show how stronger speakers deploy useful moves
in meetings, updates, stakeholder pushback, interviews, presentations, and
executive-style communication.

Date: August 10, 2026.

## Purpose

The repo already has:

- broad speaking examples
- named speaker exemplars
- general move deployment examples
- work-focused curricula and routes

This file goes narrower:

- work rooms instead of all rooms
- business pressure instead of general pressure
- decision, status, recommendation, and pushback moments instead of broad
  speaking theory

Use it when the question is not "how do I become a better speaker?" but "what
do I say in a work setting so I sound clearer, more useful, and more credible?"

If you still do not know the named move to practice, start with
`analysis/transcript-backed-concrete-speaking-approaches.md` first, then come
here for work-specific deployment versions.

## How to use this file

1. Pick the workplace moment closest to the one you actually face this week.
2. Match it to one item from
   `analysis/transcript-backed-concrete-speaking-approaches.md`.
3. Read the weak version and notice the failure mode.
4. Read the stronger version and notice what changed structurally.
5. Rewrite the stronger version in your own language.
6. Use it in one real work conversation within 72 hours.

## Example 1: Executive-style project update

### Source anchor

- `analysis/real-speaker-exemplars.md`
- `analysis/speaking-move-deployment-examples.md`
- session:
  `how-to-stop-sounding-unprofessional-at-work`

### Move

Lead with status, risk, and next step instead of reconstructing the week.

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 5: Prepare With Goal, Audience, And Constraints`
- `Approach 13: Pre-Handle Objections Before The Room Raises Them`

### Weak version

Situation:
You are updating leadership on a project that hit friction.

> "We looked at a few options last week, and there were some issues with the
> data pipeline, and design also had follow-up questions, and engineering is
> still working through a couple dependencies, so we are still trying to figure
> out the best path."

### Stronger deployment

> "Quick update: the core release is still on track, but the main risk is the
> data pipeline. My recommendation is to cut the reporting layer from this
> milestone, protect the workflow launch, and resolve the pipeline issue in
> parallel."

### Why it works

- the room knows the status quickly
- the real risk is named instead of buried
- the recommendation makes the update actionable

### Use it next in

- leadership updates
- weekly project reviews
- cross-functional launch meetings

## Example 2: Recommendation under limited attention

### Source anchor

- Guy Kawasaki in `analysis/real-speaker-exemplars.md`
- `analysis/speaking-move-deployment-examples.md`

### Move

Ask for the next decision, not total buy-in forever.

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 19: Take A Position, Give A Reason, Then Reopen`
- `Approach 22: Aim For The Next Step, Not Immediate Conversion`

### Weak version

Situation:
You are proposing a tooling change.

> "This platform really changes how everything works, and I think it could help
> every team if we adopt it broadly and move the whole organization over to it."

### Stronger deployment

> "I am not asking for an org-wide rollout today. I am asking for a three-week
> pilot with support and operations so we can measure whether it actually cuts
> handoff time before making the larger decision."

### Why it works

- the ask is proportionate
- it sounds strategic rather than overeager
- decision-makers know exactly what yes means

### Use it next in

- tool or process proposals
- internal pitches
- resource or experiment requests

## Example 3: Disagreeing with a stakeholder without sounding cornered

### Source anchor

- Sam Altman in `analysis/real-speaker-exemplars.md`
- `analysis/worked-speaking-situation-examples.md`

### Move

Acknowledge, isolate the disagreement, then offer one grounded reason.

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 3: Pause, Frame, Then Answer`
- `Approach 19: Take A Position, Give A Reason, Then Reopen`

### Weak version

Situation:
A stakeholder says the product needs more features when you think setup
friction is the bigger problem.

> "I do not really think more features are the issue. We have looked at that
> already, and changing direction now would probably just create more confusion."

### Stronger deployment

> "I see the concern. The part I read differently is the cause. I do think we
> have an adoption issue, but I think it is coming more from setup friction than
> feature depth. If useful, I can show the two signals that led me there."

### Why it works

- it lowers defensiveness in the room
- the disagreement becomes specific
- the offer of evidence keeps the tone collaborative

### Use it next in

- stakeholder reviews
- prioritization debates
- roadmap discussions

## Example 4: Answering a hard work question with incomplete information

### Source anchor

- Matt Abrahams in `analysis/real-speaker-exemplars.md`
- `analysis/speaking-move-deployment-examples.md`

### Move

State what is known, what is not known, and what you would check next.

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 3: Pause, Frame, Then Answer`
- `Approach 13: Pre-Handle Objections Before The Room Raises Them`

### Weak version

Situation:
An executive asks why churn increased and you do not yet have the full analysis.

> "There are a lot of possible reasons and we are still looking at the data, so
> I do not want to say the wrong thing until we have completed the full review."

### Stronger deployment

> "We do not have the full answer yet. What we do know is that churn rose after
> the onboarding change. The next thing I would check is whether the drop is
> concentrated in first-week users or spread across the full funnel."

### Why it works

- uncertainty is owned without collapsing
- one fact keeps the answer grounded
- the next step preserves credibility

### Use it next in

- executive Q&A
- business reviews
- postmortems

## Example 5: Interview answer that sounds credible instead of over-rehearsed

### Source anchor

- `analysis/speaking-move-deployment-examples.md`
- `analysis/worked-speaking-situation-examples.md`

### Move

Answer directly, then add the minimum context that proves judgment.

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 6: Compress Before You Expand`
- `Approach 19: Take A Position, Give A Reason, Then Reopen`

### Weak version

Situation:
An interviewer asks how you handled a project that went off track.

> "So there were a number of things going on, and I think the biggest thing is
> that there are usually many dimensions to these situations, and I tried to
> stay thoughtful about all of them..."

### Stronger deployment

> "The first thing I did was narrow the scope and protect the core deadline. We
> had too many moving pieces to recover all of them at once, so I split the
> must-ship work from the nice-to-have work and aligned the team around that."

### Why it works

- the action arrives first
- the answer sounds judged, not generic
- context supports the answer instead of replacing it

### Use it next in

- interviews
- promotion panels
- internal performance conversations

## Example 6: Opening a presentation with business relevance

### Source anchor

- Jennifer Aaker in `analysis/speaking-move-deployment-examples.md`
- `analysis/worked-speaking-situation-examples.md`

### Move

Start from a vivid business moment before abstract framing.

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 4: Use The First Five Seconds To Establish Direction`
- `Approach 18: Build The Story Around One Embodied Moment`

### Weak version

Situation:
You are presenting why a process problem matters.

> "Today I want to talk about customer experience and why operational
> reliability is important for the business."

### Stronger deployment

> "Last Thursday at 11:40 p.m., a customer spent forty minutes stuck on one
> setup step and then told support she was ready to quit. That is the operating
> problem this presentation is about."

### Why it works

- the audience sees the stakes immediately
- the abstract theme gets anchored in a scene
- attention rises before the framework appears

### Use it next in

- presentations
- roadmap reviews
- internal demos with narrative framing

## Example 7: Ending a work answer with a recommendation

### Source anchor

- Matt Abrahams and Guy Kawasaki in `analysis/real-speaker-exemplars.md`
- `analysis/business-communication-curriculum.md`

### Move

Do not stop at analysis. Land on what should happen next.

### Weak version

Situation:
You are asked what should happen after a messy launch.

> "There are a few things we should probably think through before deciding what
> to do, and there are several perspectives on what the best next step might
> be."

### Stronger deployment

> "My recommendation is that we stabilize onboarding first, pause new surface
> work for one sprint, and review retention again after that. If we do not fix
> activation, the rest of the roadmap is harder to trust."

### Why it works

- it sounds more useful
- the answer becomes decision-oriented
- the room leaves with direction instead of fog

### Use it next in

- retrospective wrap-ups
- strategy reviews
- difficult status meetings
