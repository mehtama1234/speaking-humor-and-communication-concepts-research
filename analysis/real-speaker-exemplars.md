# Real Speaker Exemplars

Source-backed examples of actual people using the speaking moves this repo keeps
teaching.

Date: August 10, 2026.

## Purpose

The repo already has:

- concepts
- drills
- situation walkthroughs
- practice routes
- session and evidence guides

This file adds a different kind of concreteness:

- real speakers
- real speaking moves
- real contexts
- adaptation rules you can borrow without copying their personality

The goal is not hero worship. It is pattern extraction.

If you already know the problem but not the exact move name, start with
`analysis/transcript-backed-concrete-speaking-approaches.md` first, then use
this file to see the move embodied by a real speaker.

## Exemplar 1: Sam Altman under an extreme accusation

### Source

- `Ultraspeaking #2 How Sam Altman Answers Impossible Questions`
- transcript:
  - `raw-material/youtube/transcripts/ultraspeaking-wave-1/clean/002-9Zhut4IIC20.txt`

### What he does

When asked a wildly loaded question, Sam does not match the emotional
temperature. He stays calm, does not over-defend, and first names what is
happening in the room: there is an accusation being made.

He also acknowledges parts of the other person's concern before moving to his
own point.

### Why it works

- he does not let the question set his emotional level
- he avoids turning the exchange into panic theater
- acknowledgment makes the other side feel heard before disagreement begins
- calmness reads as control even when the content is difficult

### What to borrow

Borrow the sequence:

- stay in character
- name the concern or frame
- acknowledge what is reasonable in the other person's position
- answer from there

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 3: Pause, Frame, Then Answer`
- `Approach 13: Pre-Handle Objections Before The Room Raises Them`

### How to use it in your life

If a stakeholder, manager, or interviewer asks something charged, do not begin
with visible scrambling.

Try:

> "I see why that would raise concern. What we know so far is..."

or:

> "That's a fair concern. The part I can answer directly is..."

### Do not copy

Do not copy Sam's tone word-for-word. Copy the control pattern:

- no panic
- no immediate over-defensiveness
- acknowledgment before countering

## Exemplar 2: Ali Abdaal recovering from tangents without sounding scripted

### Source

- `Ultraspeaking #4 How to Speak Brilliantly Well – Without a Script (Ali Abdaal)`
- transcript:
  - `raw-material/youtube/transcripts/ultraspeaking-wave-1/clean/004-i3IF86LWrKQ.txt`

### What he does

Ali goes off on tangents, then uses summary prompts such as "the point is" or
"the reason I'm saying this is" to reset both himself and the listener.

He also talks about the same core ideas at many different lengths because the
ideas are internalized rather than memorized.

### Why it works

- the audience gets the main point even if the path wandered
- summary prompts reduce visible lostness
- internalization gives him flexible fluency across formats
- he sounds alive rather than recited

### What to borrow

Borrow two things:

- summary prompts for recovery
- accordion-style practice for internalization

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 1: Talking-Points Navigation Instead Of Script Recital`
- `Approach 6: Compress Before You Expand`

### How to use it in your life

When you notice you have drifted in a meeting or answer, do not keep digging.
Reset with:

> "The point is..."

> "The short version is..."

> "What actually matters here is..."

Then practice one work topic at 30 seconds, 2 minutes, and 5 minutes. That
will make your live speaking less fragile.

### Do not copy

Do not try to sound like a YouTuber. Copy the mechanism:

- explore a little
- notice drift
- summarize cleanly

## Exemplar 3: Tristan de Montebello training speaking like a serious skill

### Source

- `Ultraspeaking #12 How Tristan de Montebello got to the World Championships of Public Speaking`
- transcript:
  - `raw-material/youtube/transcripts/ultraspeaking-wave-1/clean/012-zmGXVRBxKnE.txt`

### What he does

Tristan does not treat speaking as vague talent. He turns it into a structured
learning project:

- accelerates early reps aggressively
- studies winning examples
- gets coaching and feedback
- revises stories based on what lands
- gives many more reps than most people would

One especially strong insight from the session is that the speech improved when
the wisdom stopped coming directly from Tristan and started coming through a
mentor character inside the story.

### Why it works

- repetition creates faster adaptation
- feedback is treated like design input, not ego attack
- deliberate study shortens the path
- mentor-centered storytelling makes the lesson easier to accept

### What to borrow

Borrow the training attitude:

- more reps than feels normal
- faster feedback loops
- story revision based on audience response

Borrow the storytelling move:

- let the mentor or scene carry the wisdom, not your self-importance

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 15: Rehearse The Pressure Shape, Not Just The Content`
- `Approach 18: Build The Story Around One Embodied Moment`

### How to use it in your life

If you have a presentation in two weeks, do not just rehearse it twice in your
head.

Instead:

- record one version today
- give it to one person tomorrow
- revise the opening
- test the revised version again

If a story sounds preachy, ask:

> "Can the lesson come through another person or a concrete moment instead of me explaining it?"

## Exemplar 4: Guy Kawasaki pitching for the next step, not the instant win

### Source

- `Think Fast Talk Smart #9 How to Pitch Anything: 10 20 30 Rule by Guy Kawasaki`
- transcript:
  - `raw-material/youtube/transcripts/think-fast-talk-smart-wave-1/clean/009-HvW2cnqCVZk.txt`

### What he does

Guy reframes the purpose of a pitch. He argues that the goal is not to force an
immediate yes or "get the check" on the spot. The goal is to stay in the game
and earn the next step.

He also keeps returning to audience value rather than founder autobiography.

### Why it works

- it reduces desperate over-selling
- it keeps the speaker focused on what the audience needs to believe next
- it stops pitches from becoming self-centered life stories
- it aligns persuasion with process reality

### What to borrow

Borrow the framing:

- the goal is the next serious step
- do not get eliminated

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 22: Aim For The Next Step, Not Immediate Conversion`
- `Approach 19: Take A Position, Give A Reason, Then Reopen`

### How to use it in your life

In a recommendation, sales call, or interview, stop trying to close everything
in one burst.

Ask:

> "What is the next step I need to earn here?"

Then shape your message around that.

Examples:

- meeting: earn agreement to test the idea
- interview: earn the follow-up
- stakeholder conversation: earn permission to show evidence

## Exemplar 5: Strong conversationalists signal genuine appreciation early

### Source

- `Charisma on Command #26 How To Have Deeper Conversations With Anyone`
- transcript:
  - `raw-material/youtube/transcripts/charisma-on-command-wave-1/clean/026-BfUEDvrD1Y4.txt`

### What they do

The session highlights interviewers and conversationalists who explicitly show
they are glad to be with the other person. They do not play cool. They make
appreciation visible, then steer toward motivations, feelings, or important
relationships.

### Why it works

- people invest more when they feel welcomed
- appreciation lowers social threat
- the conversation moves out of generic small talk faster
- follow-up questions create depth without fake charisma

### What to borrow

Borrow this early sequence:

- visible appreciation
- one real follow-up
- one question about motivation, feeling, or meaning

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 8: Use Curiosity To Create Better Conversations`
- `Approach 16: Start With Connection Before Content`

### How to use it in your life

If you are meeting someone new, do not open as if indifference equals status.

Try:

> "I've been looking forward to this. I keep hearing strong things about the way you built that team."

Then ask:

> "What ended up being harder about it than people assume?"

That is much stronger than generic praise plus "so, what do you do?"

## Exemplar 6: Chris Duffy on humor that builds with people instead of performing at them

### Source

- `Think Fast Talk Smart #8 The Science of Funny: How Humor Builds Connection`
- transcript:
  - `raw-material/youtube/transcripts/think-fast-talk-smart-wave-1/clean/008-QepTzT-syro.txt`

### What he does

Chris distinguishes "jamming" from "joking." Jamming means building something
together so the other person can participate. Joking means making the moment too
centered on your own cleverness.

He also points out two practical moves:

- put the funniest word last
- leave room after the line

### Why it works

- shared play is easier to join than performance
- the other person looks good too
- timing gives the line a chance to land
- humor stops feeling like status competition

### What to borrow

Borrow the small version of this:

- notice a shared absurdity
- describe it simply
- pause after the line

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 11: Use Humor As Shared Relief, Not Performance`
- `Approach 17: Put The Turn Late And Leave Room For The Laugh`

### How to use it in your life

In a meeting or social conversation, stop trying to fire off isolated one-liners
that only prove you are witty.

Try:

> "We have somehow turned three bullets into a legend."

Then stop. Let the room react.

If someone else adds to it, build with them instead of topping them.

## Exemplar 7: Matt Abrahams using structure to make ordinary answers clearer

### Source

- `Think Fast Talk Smart #13 The First 5 Seconds: How to Start a Speech the Moment All Eyes Suddenly Turn to You | AMA`
- transcript:
  - `raw-material/youtube/transcripts/think-fast-talk-smart-wave-1/clean/013--tgPyunhoLM.txt`

### What he does

Matt introduces a simple structure, PREP:

- point
- reason
- example
- point

He models it directly instead of leaving it theoretical.

### Why it works

- it gives pressure answers shape fast
- examples make abstract points easier to remember
- restating the point reduces drift
- it helps people defend a position without rambling

### What to borrow

Borrow PREP when you need to advocate, recommend, or defend something.

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 6: Compress Before You Expand`
- `Approach 19: Take A Position, Give A Reason, Then Reopen`

### How to use it in your life

If your manager asks why a plan should change, answer like this:

- point: "We should delay the launch by one week."
- reason: "The current onboarding bug will distort first impressions."
- example: "Three user tests failed at the same step."
- point: "So delaying one week is lower risk than launching now."

This is cleaner than thinking out loud for 90 seconds.

## Exemplar 8: Vinh Giang teaching direct work credibility through message shape

### Source

- `Vinh Giang #15 How To Stop Sounding Unprofessional At Work`
- related repo route:
  - `analysis/business-communication-curriculum.md`
  - `analysis/live-speaking-phrase-bank.md`

### What he does

Vinh repeatedly pushes speakers away from apology-heavy, low-status, context
sprawling delivery and toward cleaner recommendation-shaped speech.

The broader pattern across the repo is consistent:

- fewer softeners
- clearer bottom lines
- more direct ownership
- better audience processing

### Why it works

- professional credibility is often shaped before the content is deeply judged
- people trust speakers who make relevance legible
- cleaner message shape sounds more senior

### What to borrow

Borrow the professional message pattern:

- bottom line
- supporting reason
- next step

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 5: Prepare With Goal, Audience, And Constraints`
- `Approach 7: Remove Low-Status Leakage`

### How to use it in your life

Instead of:

> "I just wanted to quickly mention a couple things we might maybe think about..."

Try:

> "My recommendation is to cut scope on the dashboard and protect the core release. The risk is concentrated in the pipeline work, and this is the fastest way to ship safely."
