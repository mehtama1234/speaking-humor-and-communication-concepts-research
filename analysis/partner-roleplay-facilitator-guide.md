# Partner Roleplay Facilitator Guide

Concrete instructions for two people running speaking roleplays together
without turning the exercise into vague encouragement or random improvisation.

Date: August 10, 2026.

## Purpose

The repo already has:

- `analysis/speaking-roleplay-packs.md`
- `analysis/roleplay-feedback-scorecard.md`
- `analysis/listener-feedback-template.md`
- `analysis/real-life-speaking-simulations.md`

What it still needed was a cleaner partner-run practice layer:

- who plays which role
- how hard the follow-up should be
- when to escalate
- how to score the rep
- how to avoid useless feedback

This file gives the repo a more coachable and repeatable two-person mode.

If the pair knows the scenario but not the exact move to pressure-test, start
with `analysis/transcript-backed-concrete-speaking-approaches.md` first, then
use this guide to keep the roleplay centered on that named behavior.

## When to use this

Use this when:

- one learner has a practice partner
- a teammate or friend is helping run speaking reps
- you want stronger practice than solo answers but lighter structure than a
  full coaching session

It is especially useful for:

- interviews
- stakeholder pushback
- work updates under challenge
- presentation Q&A
- social conversation depth
- humor and awkward-recovery reps

## The two roles

### Role 1: Speaker

The speaker's job is to:

- answer the prompt directly
- protect the main move under pressure
- stay in the scenario instead of explaining the exercise

The main move should come from:

- `analysis/transcript-backed-concrete-speaking-approaches.md`

The speaker should not:

- stop every ten seconds to ask how it sounded
- defend the rep after each line
- ask the facilitator to make it easier the moment it gets uncomfortable

### Role 2: Facilitator

The facilitator's job is to:

- read the scenario cleanly
- deliver the escalation turn with believable pressure
- score what actually happened
- keep the exercise honest and useful

The facilitator should not:

- over-help mid-answer
- soften every follow-up into nothing
- pile on five follow-ups in one rep
- give vague praise instead of concrete feedback

## Standard two-person format

Use this as the default structure.

### Round 1: Cold rep

1. Facilitator reads the situation exactly once.
2. Speaker gives the first answer without notes.
3. Facilitator delivers the escalation turn.
4. Speaker gives the second answer.
5. Both stop.

Goal:

- reveal the real failure mode before trying to fix it

### Round 2: Focused rep

1. Facilitator names one target:
   - first sentence
   - composure
   - second-turn clarity
   - warmth
   - usefulness
   - or one exact move from
     `analysis/transcript-backed-concrete-speaking-approaches.md`
2. Speaker runs the same scenario again.
3. Facilitator gives the same escalation turn again.
4. Speaker answers again.

Goal:

- improve one dimension, not everything

### Round 3: Harder rep

1. Facilitator raises the realism slightly.
2. Speaker runs the scenario again.
3. Facilitator gives a sharper version of the follow-up.
4. Speaker answers again.

Goal:

- test whether the improvement survives slightly more pressure

## How the facilitator should escalate

Escalation should feel realistic, not theatrical.

Good escalation adds one of these:

- more direct skepticism
- less patience
- a request for evidence
- a misunderstanding that must be clarified
- a social reply that invites depth instead of surface chatter

Bad escalation usually looks like:

- making the other person absurdly hostile
- asking three unrelated questions at once
- turning a social rep into an interrogation
- changing the topic completely

## Escalation ladder

Use this ladder when you want a more structured rise in difficulty.

### Level 1: Clean follow-up

Use the roleplay pack exactly as written.

Best for:

- first rep
- newer learners
- diagnosis

### Level 2: Sharper follow-up

Ask the same core question but with a little more pressure.

Example:

- from:
  `Why wasn't that visible earlier?`
- to:
  `Why did nobody hear about that until it became a delay?`

Best for:

- second rep
- pressure tolerance
- realism without chaos

### Level 3: Misunderstanding or challenge

Add one realistic misread that forces the speaker to clarify.

Example:

- `So are you saying the feature doesn't matter?`

Best for:

- stakeholder disagreement
- interviews
- presentations

## Best facilitator prompts by scenario type

### Work update or project defense

Use prompts like:

- `Why was that not visible earlier?`
- `What changes now?`
- `What decision do you actually need from me?`
Best exact approaches:
- `Approach 5`, `Approach 13`, `Approach 19`

### Interview or hard question

Use prompts like:

- `What should you have done differently?`
- `How do you know that now?`
- `What would you check next before deciding?`
Best exact approaches:
- `Approach 3`, `Approach 15`

### Stakeholder disagreement

Use prompts like:

- `Are you saying the feature does not matter?`
- `Why is your interpretation stronger than mine?`
- `What evidence are you leaning on?`
Best exact approaches:
- `Approach 3`, `Approach 19`

### Social depth

Use prompts like:

- `A little of both. We just finished a launch that took over my life.`
- `Honestly I am still figuring that out.`
- `That sounds obvious. What do you really mean?`
Best exact approaches:
- `Approach 8`, `Approach 16`

### Humor

Use prompts like:

- a simple laugh and one added line
- a playful continuation
- a flat reaction if the joke was forced
Best exact approaches:
- `Approach 11`, `Approach 17`

The goal is not to crush the speaker. The goal is to reveal whether the move
was real.

## How to score the rep

After each round, the facilitator should use:

- `analysis/roleplay-feedback-scorecard.md`

Do not score every category deeply every time.

Pick the two or three that matter most for the scenario:

- work update:
  - first-sentence clarity
  - credibility and usefulness
  - second-turn resilience
- interview:
  - pressure composure
  - first-sentence clarity
  - second-turn resilience
- stakeholder disagreement:
  - relational control
  - second-turn resilience
  - usefulness
- social conversation:
  - warmth
  - usefulness
- humor:
  - warmth
  - second-turn resilience

## Feedback rules for the facilitator

Good feedback is:

- brief
- specific
- tied to one visible moment
- aimed at the next rep

Good examples:

- `Your first sentence was clearer, but the second answer got defensive.`
- `You answered the concern, but you still buried the recommendation.`
- `The social follow-up got better once you stopped switching back to yourself.`
- `The joke worked when you left it alone. It weakened when you explained it.`

Bad feedback sounds like:

- `That was better.`
- `I don't know, maybe just be more confident.`
- `You sounded weird.`
- `I liked the second one more.`

## Partner note template

Use this after a three-round block:

```text
Scenario:

Round 1 failure mode:

One move we were trying to protect:

Best round:

Weakest category on the scorecard:

What changed between Round 1 and Round 3:

What to repeat next time:
```

## Best 20-minute partner session

Use this structure:

1. 2 minutes:
   pick one roleplay and one target category
2. 4 minutes:
   Round 1 cold rep
3. 4 minutes:
   Round 2 focused rep
4. 4 minutes:
   Round 3 harder rep
5. 3 minutes:
   score only the key categories
6. 3 minutes:
   write one takeaway and one next rep
