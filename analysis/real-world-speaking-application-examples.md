# Real World Speaking Application Examples

Concrete examples of actual people using speaking moves well, paired with the
real situations where you should use the same move and a stronger version of
what you could actually say.

Date: August 10, 2026.

## Purpose

Some files in this repo already cover:

- source-backed speaker exemplars
- weak versus stronger before-and-after examples
- practice recipes and drills
- roleplays and live missions

This file is narrower and more practical.

It answers:

- who is using the move
- what exactly they are doing
- where that same move belongs in ordinary life
- what a weak version sounds like
- what a stronger borrowed version sounds like

Use this file when the bottleneck is not theory. The bottleneck is:

> "Show me an actual person doing it, then show me exactly how I would use it
> in my own next situation."

If the learner still does not know the exact move name yet, start with
`analysis/transcript-backed-concrete-speaking-approaches.md` first, then use
this file to see the move embodied in a real speaker and a real room.

## How to use this file

1. Pick one situation you are likely to face in the next seven days.
2. Match it to one item from
   `analysis/transcript-backed-concrete-speaking-approaches.md`.
3. Study the person only long enough to identify the move.
4. Read the weak and stronger adaptation out loud.
5. Rewrite the stronger version into your own vocabulary.
6. Use it in one live conversation, meeting, interview, or presentation within
   72 hours.

## Example 1: Sam Altman under accusation -> stakeholder pressure at work

### Real observed example

Source:

- `Ultraspeaking #2 How Sam Altman Answers Impossible Questions`
- transcript:
  `raw-material/youtube/transcripts/ultraspeaking-wave-1/clean/002-9Zhut4IIC20.txt`

Observed move:

- he does not match the emotional heat of the question
- he names the concern instead of acting personally wounded by it
- he acknowledges the reasonable part before answering

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 3: Pause, Frame, Then Answer`
- `Approach 13: Pre-Handle Objections Before The Room Raises Them`

### What this looks like in your life

Situation:
A manager says, "Why did this slip again if we already talked about the risk
last week?"

Weak answer:

> "There were just a lot of moving pieces and dependencies and we were still
> figuring some things out, so it was more complicated than it probably looked."

Stronger adaptation:

> "I see why that is frustrating. The main reason it slipped is that the vendor
> dependency stayed unresolved longer than expected. My recommendation is that
> we cut the lower-priority export work and protect the core deadline."

### Why this transfer works

- calmness prevents the room from escalating further
- acknowledgment reduces unnecessary combat
- one cause plus one recommendation sounds credible

### Use it next in

- a project review
- a sharp interview follow-up
- a tense leadership question

## Example 2: Ali Abdaal resetting after drift -> messy meeting answer

### Real observed example

Source:

- `Ultraspeaking #4 How to Speak Brilliantly Well – Without a Script (Ali Abdaal)`
- transcript:
  `raw-material/youtube/transcripts/ultraspeaking-wave-1/clean/004-i3IF86LWrKQ.txt`

Observed move:

- he allows some live exploration
- when the answer starts drifting, he resets with a summary phrase
- he sounds alive because the idea is internalized, not recited

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 1: Talking-Points Navigation Instead Of Script Recital`
- `Approach 6: Compress Before You Expand`

### What this looks like in your life

Situation:
You are asked, "What did we actually learn from the customer interviews?"

Weak answer:

> "There were a few themes, and reporting came up, and onboarding came up, and
> one customer had a totally different use case that may also connect to
> pricing, so there are a few directions we could explore."

Stronger adaptation:

> "The short version is that onboarding is the real blocker. Reporting came up,
> but mostly as a second-order issue. If we reduce setup friction first, most of
> the rest becomes easier to solve."

### Why this transfer works

- the summary line rescues the answer without panic
- the listener gets hierarchy instead of a pile of observations
- you sound thoughtful rather than over-scripted

### Use it next in

- a cross-functional meeting
- an interview answer that starts sprawling
- a panel or podcast response

## Example 3: Guy Kawasaki asking for the next step -> internal buy-in ask

### Real observed example

Source:

- `Think Fast Talk Smart #9 How to Pitch Anything: 10 20 30 Rule by Guy Kawasaki`
- transcript:
  `raw-material/youtube/transcripts/think-fast-talk-smart-wave-1/clean/009-HvW2cnqCVZk.txt`

Observed move:

- he reframes persuasion around earning the next step
- he avoids the desperate need to win everything immediately
- he keeps the ask proportionate

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 22: Aim For The Next Step, Not Immediate Conversion`
- `Approach 19: Take A Position, Give A Reason, Then Reopen`

### What this looks like in your life

Situation:
You want your company to try a new workflow tool.

Weak answer:

> "This could completely transform how the whole team works, and I think we
> should roll it out broadly as soon as possible."

Stronger adaptation:

> "I am not asking for a full rollout today. I am asking for a two-team pilot
> over the next three weeks so we can see whether it cuts handoff time enough
> to justify a broader rollout."

### Why this transfer works

- the room hears a credible ask instead of sales inflation
- resistance drops because the decision is reversible
- you sound commercially realistic

### Use it next in

- an internal pitch
- a founder or product recommendation
- a request for resources or budget

## Example 4: Tristan de Montebello revising for what lands -> better story prep

### Real observed example

Source:

- `Ultraspeaking #12 How Tristan de Montebello got to the World Championships of Public Speaking`
- transcript:
  `raw-material/youtube/transcripts/ultraspeaking-wave-1/clean/012-zmGXVRBxKnE.txt`

Observed move:

- he treats speaking as a trainable craft
- he uses feedback, repetition, and revision
- he lets the scene or mentor carry the lesson instead of preaching directly

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 18: Build The Story Around One Embodied Moment`
- `Approach 15: Rehearse The Pressure Shape, Not Just The Content`

### What this looks like in your life

Situation:
You have a presentation story that sounds worthy but flat.

Weak answer:

> "The lesson I learned is that resilience matters and that we should all keep
> going even when things are hard."

Stronger adaptation:

> "My former manager looked at the broken release, stayed calm, and said,
> 'Let us fix what customers are touching first.' That moment changed how I make
> decisions under pressure."

### Why this transfer works

- a scene is easier to believe than a moral lecture
- another person can carry the wisdom without sounding self-important
- the audience remembers moments better than lessons

### Use it next in

- a presentation opening
- a behavioral interview story
- a team retrospective

## Example 5: Matt Abrahams going point-first -> stronger executive update

### Real observed example

Source:

- `Think Fast Talk Smart: Know Before You Tell - The Three-Part Framework Every Communicator Needs`
- transcript:
  `raw-material/youtube/transcripts/think-fast-talk-smart-wave-1/clean/014-5KjQV3GNG6s.txt`

Observed move:

- he keeps returning to goal, audience, and data
- he does not bury the point in background
- he frames information around usefulness to the listener

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 5: Prepare With Goal, Audience, And Constraints`
- `Approach 13: Pre-Handle Objections Before The Room Raises Them`

### What this looks like in your life

Situation:
You need to give a short update to leadership.

Weak answer:

> "We looked at a few options last week, had some design questions, and there
> are still dependencies in engineering, so we are working through all of that
> right now."

Stronger adaptation:

> "Quick update: we are still on track for Friday, but the main risk is the
> data pipeline. I recommend we cut the low-priority dashboard piece and ship
> the core workflow first."

### Why this transfer works

- the room hears status, risk, and recommendation quickly
- leaders do not have to excavate the point
- usefulness replaces narrative clutter

### Use it next in

- a weekly update
- a steering meeting
- an executive check-in

## Example 6: Chris Duffy using shared absurdity -> humor that actually lands

### Real observed example

Source:

- `Think Fast Talk Smart: The Science of Funny - How Humor Builds Connection`
- transcript:
  `raw-material/youtube/transcripts/think-fast-talk-smart-wave-1/clean/008-QepTzT-syro.txt`

Observed move:

- humor comes from noticing what is already funny in shared reality
- the line is collaborative, not a performance audition
- he does not over-explain the joke

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 11: Use Humor As Shared Relief, Not Performance`
- `Approach 17: Put The Turn Late And Leave Room For The Laugh`

### What this looks like in your life

Situation:
A meeting has become overcomplicated and everyone can feel it.

Weak answer:

> "I am bad at jokes, but this meeting could have been an email."

Stronger adaptation:

> "We somehow started with three bullets and built a full cinematic universe."

### Why this transfer works

- it uses the room's shared reality
- it creates an image fast
- it adds relief without becoming attention-seeking

### Use it next in

- long team meetings
- offsites
- group hangs with obvious shared absurdity

## Example 8: Direct awkward repair -> social recovery without spiral

### Real observed example

Source:

- `Charisma on Command: What To Say When A Conversation Gets Awkward`
- transcript:
  `raw-material/youtube/transcripts/charisma-on-command-wave-1/clean/040-_2tXNCxg8hs.txt`

Observed move:

- repair the awkwardness directly
- clarify intent briefly
- do not ask the other person to manage your embarrassment

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 9: Repair Awkwardness By Naming And Redirecting`

### What this looks like in your life

Situation:
You make a line that sounds colder than you intended.

Weak answer:

> "Oh wow, sorry, I am awkward sometimes, forget I said that."

Stronger adaptation:

> "That came out more dismissive than I meant. What I was trying to say is that
> you handled that much more calmly than most people would."

### Why this transfer works

- ownership stabilizes the interaction fast
- brief repair feels mature
- the conversation can move forward instead of collapsing

### Use it next in

- dates
- group conversations
- work conversations where tone landed wrong

## Example 9: Andrew Tarvin filtering humor before it causes damage -> safer
## tension release at work

### Real observed example

Source:

- `Andrew Tarvin #28 The Dangers of Humor at Work`
- transcript:
  `raw-material/youtube/transcripts/andrew-tarvin-wave-1/clean/028-iHA7_tbx8S8.txt`

Observed move:

- he does not treat all laughter as a win
- he checks humor against three failure modes:
  distract, divide, disparage
- he keeps humor tied to the work and inclusive of the room

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 11: Use Humor As Shared Relief, Not Performance`
- `Approach 22: Aim For The Next Step, Not Immediate Conversion`

### What this looks like in your life

Situation:
Your team is frustrated in a postmortem and you want to lighten the room.

Weak answer:

> "Well, at least now we know who to blame for this disaster."

Stronger adaptation:

> "We managed to turn one bug into a full cross-functional field trip, so let
> us at least make sure the return ticket is a cleaner fix and owner list."

### Why this transfer works

- the line comments on the process instead of humiliating a person
- it releases tension without creating an in-group and an out-group
- it brings the room back to the next useful step

### Use it next in

- retrospectives
- tough project reviews
- tense status meetings

## Example 10: Andrew Tarvin pacing for virtual laughter -> cleaner online
## emceeing and workshop delivery

### Real observed example

Source:

- `Andrew Tarvin #29 How To Hold A Successful Virtual Event | Virtual Emceeing | Humor That Works`
- transcript:
  `raw-material/youtube/transcripts/andrew-tarvin-wave-1/clean/029-NrN9iNPOi8w.txt`

Observed move:

- he anticipates delay instead of reading silence as failure
- he adds pause so the audience can process and react
- he warns that over-explaining the joke usually kills it

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 17: Put The Turn Late And Leave Room For The Laugh`
- `Approach 21: Use Unhurried Pace To Claim Space`

### What this looks like in your life

Situation:
You are hosting a virtual all-hands and want to use one light line before the
next segment.

Weak answer:

> "Looks like finance is here, which means the fun police arrived, haha, just
> kidding, what I mean is they care about details and budgets and..."

Stronger adaptation:

> "Finance is here, which means our reckless creativity now has adult
> supervision."

### Why this transfer works

- the line is short enough to survive virtual delay
- the pause after it gives the room a chance to react
- you avoid draining the joke by explaining it to death

### Use it next in

- virtual town halls
- webinars
- workshop transitions
