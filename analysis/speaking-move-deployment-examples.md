# Speaking Move Deployment Examples

Concrete deployment examples that connect a named speaker, a specific move, and
the kind of real situation where you should actually use it.

Date: August 10, 2026.

## Purpose

Several files in this repo already help with adjacent parts of the problem:

- `analysis/real-speaker-exemplars.md` shows named speakers using strong moves
- `analysis/worked-speaking-situation-examples.md` shows weak versus stronger
  wording in realistic moments
- `analysis/speaker-pattern-playbook.md` extracts reusable move patterns
- `analysis/speaking-roleplay-packs.md` lets you practice harder second turns

This file adds one more layer:

- who used the move
- what the move was doing
- what a weak real-life use looks like
- what a stronger real-life use looks like
- where you should try it next

The point is deployment, not admiration.

If you already know the room but not the named move, open
`analysis/transcript-backed-concrete-speaking-approaches.md` first. This file
works best once you have a candidate move and want to see it deployed in a
real-life version.

## How to use this file

1. Pick one situation you are likely to face in the next seven days.
2. Match it to one item from
   `analysis/transcript-backed-concrete-speaking-approaches.md`.
3. Study the named speaker only long enough to see the move clearly.
4. Read the weak and stronger versions out loud.
5. Rewrite the stronger version into your own vocabulary.
6. Use it in one live interaction within 48 hours.

## Example 1: Sam Altman and charged questions

### Source anchor

- `analysis/real-speaker-exemplars.md`
- `analysis/speaker-pattern-playbook.md`
- transcript:
  `raw-material/youtube/transcripts/ultraspeaking-wave-1/clean/002-9Zhut4IIC20.txt`

### Move

Do not match the heat of the question. Acknowledge the concern, then answer
from stable ground.

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 3: Pause, Frame, Then Answer`
- `Approach 13: Pre-Handle Objections Before The Room Raises Them`

### Weak real-life version

Situation:
Your manager asks, "Why did this slip again if we already discussed the risks
last week?"

Weak answer:

> "Yeah, I know, and we have been looking at a lot of things and there were
> several moving parts and engineering was blocked on some dependencies, so it
> was not really as simple as just doing what we said last week."

### Stronger deployment

> "I see why that is frustrating. The main reason for the slip is that the
> vendor dependency stayed unresolved longer than expected. The fix I am
> proposing is to cut the lower-priority export work and protect the core
> deadline."

### Why this is the right use

- it acknowledges pressure without collapsing
- it names one real cause instead of spraying context
- it moves toward a recommendation

### Use it here this week

- a tense project review
- a hard interview follow-up
- a stakeholder challenge in a meeting

### Do not misuse it

Do not turn calmness into vagueness. Calm only works if the answer still says
something definite.

## Example 2: Ali Abdaal and tangent recovery

### Source anchor

- `analysis/real-speaker-exemplars.md`
- `analysis/worked-speaking-situation-examples.md`
- transcript:
  `raw-material/youtube/transcripts/ultraspeaking-wave-1/clean/004-i3IF86LWrKQ.txt`

### Move

When you drift, reset with a summary line instead of wandering longer.

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 1: Talking-Points Navigation Instead Of Script Recital`
- `Approach 6: Compress Before You Expand`

### Weak real-life version

Situation:
You are answering, "What did we learn from the customer interviews?"

Weak answer:

> "There were a few things. Some people mentioned onboarding, and then there
> were comments about reporting, and one team had this very different use case,
> which was interesting because it kind of connects to the earlier pricing
> discussion, and maybe also to activation..."

### Stronger deployment

> "The short version is that onboarding is the real blocker. Reporting came up,
> but mostly as a second-order issue. If we reduce setup friction, I think the
> rest of the feedback becomes much easier to act on."

### Why this is the right use

- it rescues a messy answer without pretending the tangent never happened
- it gives the listener a hierarchy
- it sounds more thoughtful than polished-for-show

### Use it here this week

- a meeting update when you feel yourself drifting
- a podcast or panel answer that starts sprawling
- an interview answer that got too broad

### Do not misuse it

Do not stack five summary phrases in one answer. One reset is strong. Repeated
resets sound like you are losing the thread.

## Example 3: Guy Kawasaki and next-step persuasion

### Source anchor

- `analysis/real-speaker-exemplars.md`
- `analysis/speaker-patterns-by-context.md`
- transcript:
  `raw-material/youtube/transcripts/think-fast-talk-smart-wave-1/clean/009-HvW2cnqCVZk.txt`

### Move

Pitch for the next step, not for instant total conversion.

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 19: Take A Position, Give A Reason, Then Reopen`
- `Approach 22: Aim For The Next Step, Not Immediate Conversion`

### Weak real-life version

Situation:
You are trying to get buy-in for a new internal tool.

Weak answer:

> "This will transform the way the whole team works. It solves everything we
> have been struggling with, and I really think we should commit to rolling it
> out across every function right away."

### Stronger deployment

> "I am not asking for a full rollout today. I am asking for a two-team pilot
> over the next three weeks so we can see whether it cuts handoff time enough to
> justify a broader rollout."

### Why this is the right use

- it lowers resistance
- it matches how decisions usually get made
- it sounds more credible because the ask is proportionate

### Use it here this week

- asking for a pilot
- pitching a project in a meeting
- proposing a new process to leadership

### Do not misuse it

Do not make the ask so small that it becomes meaningless. The next step still
needs to matter.

## Example 4: Charisma-on-Command style awkward repair

### Source anchor

- `analysis/worked-speaking-situation-examples.md`
- `analysis/speaker-pattern-playbook.md`
- session:
  `what-to-say-when-a-conversation-gets-awkward`

### Move

Repair the awkward moment directly, then return to the conversation.

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 9: Repair Awkwardness By Naming And Redirecting`

### Weak real-life version

Situation:
You make a comment that sounds dismissive in a group conversation.

Weak answer:

> "Oh no, sorry, I did not mean it like that at all, I am really bad at wording
> things sometimes, forget I said that."

### Stronger deployment

> "That came out more dismissive than I meant. What I was trying to say is that
> you handled a messy situation more calmly than most people would."

### Why this is the right use

- it owns the miss cleanly
- it does not make the other person manage your panic
- it clarifies intent fast enough to save the interaction

### Use it here this week

- a team discussion where you phrase something badly
- a date or social conversation with a misread joke
- a networking conversation where your wording lands cold

### Do not misuse it

Do not keep explaining after the repair lands. Repair, clarify, move on.

## Example 5: Matt Abrahams and point-first work communication

### Source anchor

- `analysis/worked-speaking-situation-examples.md`
- `analysis/speaker-patterns-by-context.md`
- session:
  `know-before-you-tell-the-three-part-framework-every-communicator-needs`

### Move

Lead with the point, then add the minimum support needed.

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 5: Prepare With Goal, Audience, And Constraints`
- `Approach 13: Pre-Handle Objections Before The Room Raises Them`

### Weak real-life version

Situation:
You are giving a project update to senior people with limited patience.

Weak answer:

> "So last week we reviewed a few directions and then we had some follow-up
> questions from design, and the data issue is still being investigated, and we
> also started thinking through whether we may need to sequence the launch
> differently..."

### Stronger deployment

> "The decision I need today is whether we protect the Friday launch by cutting
> the low-priority reporting layer. The reason is that the data issue is real,
> but it only blocks the reporting piece, not the core workflow."

### Why this is the right use

- it respects audience attention
- it makes the ask audible
- it turns an update into usable communication

### Use it here this week

- leadership updates
- standups that keep running long
- client or stakeholder reviews

### Do not misuse it

Do not go point-first and then dump ten minutes of background anyway. The point
has to stay the center of gravity.

## Example 6: Jennifer Aaker and story-led openings

### Source anchor

- `analysis/worked-speaking-situation-examples.md`
- `analysis/watch-before-you-practice-guide.md`
- session:
  `transform-your-storytelling-by-understanding-these-three-layers`

### Move

Open with a concrete moment before abstract explanation.

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 18: Build The Story Around One Embodied Moment`
- `Approach 4: Use The First Five Seconds To Establish Direction`

### Weak real-life version

Situation:
You are presenting why a user problem matters.

Weak answer:

> "Today I want to talk about customer empathy and why support quality matters
> across the product lifecycle."

### Stronger deployment

> "At 11:40 p.m. last Thursday, a customer sent a message saying she had spent
> forty minutes trying to complete one setup step and was ready to give up. That
> is the support problem we are actually trying to solve."

### Why this is the right use

- the audience sees the problem before being told the theme
- the abstraction has somewhere to land
- attention rises because the opening contains a scene

### Use it here this week

- presentation openings
- persuasive internal memos you present live
- stories during interviews or team retrospectives

### Do not misuse it

Do not add fake drama. The scene should clarify the point, not cosplay
importance.

## Example 7: Humor built from shared reality

### Source anchor

- `analysis/worked-speaking-situation-examples.md`
- `analysis/social-speaking-and-humor-curriculum.md`
- session:
  `the-science-of-funny-how-humor-builds-connection`

### Move

Use humor to heighten a shared reality, not to audition for approval.

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 11: Use Humor As Shared Relief, Not Performance`
- `Approach 17: Put The Turn Late And Leave Room For The Laugh`

### Weak real-life version

Situation:
The meeting is dragging and you want to add lightness.

Weak answer:

> "I am bad at jokes, but I guess this meeting could have been an email."

### Stronger deployment

> "We somehow started with three bullets and built a full extended universe."

### Why this is the right use

- it is based on a shared experience in the room
- it adds emotional relief without attacking anyone
- it does not sound needy or pre-apologized

### Use it here this week

- a long meeting that needs a pressure release
- social conversation with a visible shared absurdity
- collaborative work sessions where warmth matters

### Do not misuse it

Do not explain the joke after saying it. If it lands, move on. If it misses,
move on even faster.

## Example 8: Curiosity-first warmth in conversation

### Source anchor

- `analysis/real-speaker-exemplars.md`
- `analysis/speaker-pattern-playbook.md`
- session:
  `hello-stranger-why-curiosity-beats-charisma-every-time`

### Move

Offer one human detail, then ask a question with texture.

Exact approach match:

- `Approach 8: Use Curiosity To Create Better Conversations`
- `Approach 16: Start With Connection Before Content`

### Weak real-life version

Situation:
Someone asks how your week is going at a social event.

Weak answer:

> "Busy. Same old stuff. How about you?"

### Stronger deployment

> "Busy, but in a good way. I have been trying to fix one part of our product
> that everyone uses and nobody notices unless it breaks. What has been taking
> most of your energy lately?"

### Why this is the right use

- it gives the other person something real to respond to
- the follow-up question invites more than a surface answer
- warmth comes from interest, not performance

### Use it here this week

- networking events
- dates
- team offsites
- any conversation where you want more depth than polite filler

### Do not misuse it

Do not turn textured curiosity into interrogation. Offer a little of yourself
before asking for more from the other person.
