## What this slice appears to cover best

This first slice is strongest on:

- translating humor into a work-safe communication skill rather than treating
  it as entertainment performance
- using presentation craft, iteration, and audience engagement to hold attention
- clarifying the mechanics behind comedy structures like the `comic triple`
- distinguishing inclusive, helpful humor from distracting, divisive, or
  disparaging humor
- using improv-style games to build spontaneity and faster recovery
- connecting humor to business storytelling and message memorability

It is weaker, at least in this first slice, on:

- long-form everyday social conversation compared with `Chad Littlefield`
- conflict and disagreement language compared with `Jefferson Fisher`
- polished exemplar speeches compared with `Toastmasters International`
- emotional storytelling depth compared with `Matthew Dicks`

## Preliminary theme arcs

### 1. Humor is most valuable when it helps the work, not when it hijacks it

This corpus repeatedly frames humor as a communication tool that should help
attention, trust, and memory rather than become an unrelated gimmick.

Strong anchors:

### 2. Work-safe humor depends on inclusion, not cheap laughs

One of the most useful ideas in this source is that humor fails when it
creates the wrong group boundary. Andrew repeatedly distinguishes positive,
inclusive humor from distracting, divisive, or disparaging humor.

Strong anchors:

- `#28 The Dangers of Humor at Work`
- `#18 What is Blue Humor?`
- `#40 "Why Don't You Laugh?" Lincoln and the Importance of Humor`

This matters because the repo has needed stronger guidance on what not to do
with humor in professional rooms, not only how to add humor.

Best exact-approach bridges:

- `Approach 11`
- `Approach 22`
